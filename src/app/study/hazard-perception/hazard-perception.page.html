Hazard Perception Hazard Perception

A quick note on what this is (and isn't): the real DVSA hazard perception test uses video clips, which isn't something this app can generate. What follows is a written and diagram-based guide to the underlying skill the test measures — spotting a developing situation early — plus what to expect on the day. For actual clip practice, use the official practice materials on gov.uk.

How the real test works

You're shown video clips filmed from a car's point of view, and you click or tap as soon as you spot a developing hazard — something that would make a competent driver need to take action. The earlier you spot it within the scoring window, the higher your mark; react too late and you may score nothing for it.

There are usually 14 clips, one of which typically contains two separate developing hazards, giving 15 scoring opportunities. The pass mark has generally been around 44 out of a possible 75 — but formats and pass marks can change, so check the current details on gov.uk rather than relying on this figure.

One important trap: the system is designed to catch clicking repeatedly or rhythmically to try to "cover" every possible hazard — this can zero your score for that clip even with otherwise good timing. Watch genuinely, and click when you actually see something developing.

Developing hazard vs potential hazard

A potential hazard could turn into a problem but hasn't started to yet — a ball lying still on a pavement, a pedestrian standing at the kerb but not moving.

A developing hazard is the same situation once it starts to actually affect your driving — the ball starts rolling into the road, the pedestrian steps off the kerb. This is the moment the real test scores: not "is there anything here", but "has this started to require a response".

Worked example: a residential street

  1. Gap between parked cars. A common place for a pedestrian, especially a child, to step out without warning. Worth covering the brake and easing off before you draw level with it.
  2. A ball near the gap. On its own it's only a potential hazard. If it starts moving, or you spot the child likely to follow it, that's when it becomes a developing hazard.
  3. Brake lights on a car further ahead. A clue that something up there is slowing traffic — start easing off in anticipation rather than waiting for the car directly ahead to react first.

Worked example: a side junction

  1. A car waiting at the give-way line. Its front is just visible past the line. It may already be looking for a gap in traffic — including the gap right where you are.
  2. A cyclist on your road, closer to the junction than the waiting car. A driver pulling out might see your car but misjudge the cyclist's speed or miss them entirely — a classic cause of junction collisions.
  3. Your own position and speed. With two things to track at once, easing off slightly and covering the brake gives you time to react to whichever one actually moves.

Scanning technique

  • Keep your eyes moving — near, far, side to side — rather than fixing on one point.
  • Look well beyond the vehicle directly ahead, towards the next junction or brake lights further down the road.
  • Check both sides of the road, not just the direction you expect a hazard from.
  • Pay particular attention around junctions, parked vehicles, bus stops, crossings and schools.
  • In poor visibility, you'll see hazards later than usual — slow down accordingly.

Common mistakes

  • Clicking repeatedly or rhythmically to try to "cover" every possibility — this can zero your score.
  • Reacting to the first obvious hazard and missing a second, quieter one developing elsewhere.
  • Tunnel vision under pressure — narrowing attention to directly ahead and missing hazards from the sides.
  • Waiting for total certainty before reacting, instead of responding as a situation starts to develop.
